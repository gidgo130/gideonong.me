Reading Your Fits · Module 4

Time-Ordered Data

A data logger can record a thousand samples a minute. That doesn't give you a thousand independent pieces of evidence, and your error bars don't know it.

When each reading is tied to the one before it (filtering, slow dynamics, drift), the errors are autocorrelated: the leftovers come in runs instead of scattering independently. The fitted line survives. Its error bars, and anything you fit after a log transform, may not. All data here is simulated.

What to take away

  1. Always plot residuals in the order you took them. Runs of the same sign mean the errors are linked, and the error bars are too narrow.
  2. Fix the model first. Autocorrelated residuals often mean missing dynamics: a drift term, a second time constant, a warm-up. Then use Newey–West (HAC) standard errors for what's left.
  3. Don't log-linearize a decay to fit it. Fit the exponential directly (curve_fit, fitnlm, Solver). The same goes for log decrement on noisy ringdowns: use many cycles or fit the damped sine.
  4. Count runs, not samples, when runs differ. More samples per run don't shrink run-to-run uncertainty; more runs do.

Checks in your software

Excel

Plot residuals against row number. Lag-1 correlation: =CORREL(D3:D101, D2:D100).

MATLAB

dwtest(mdl) for Durbin–Watson, autocorr(res) for the lag plot, fitnlm for the exponential.

Python

statsmodels durbin_watson, plot_acf, fit(cov_type='HAC', cov_kwds={'maxlags': L}); scipy curve_fit for the exponential.
