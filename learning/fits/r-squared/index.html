Reading Your Fits · Module 1

Why R² Isn't Enough

R² is the number everyone reports and almost nobody should trust on its own. It can't tell a good fit from a bad one, it rises when you test a wider range, and it always goes up when you add terms.

R² is the share of the scatter in y that the fitted line accounts for: 1 means every point sits on the line, 0 means the line explains nothing. Excel's trendline shows it by default. This module shows three ways it misleads. Trials 2 and 3 use simulated data with a known true line.

What to take away

  1. R² can't tell a good fit from a bad one. Four very different datasets share R² = 0.67. Look at the data and the residuals.
  2. R² depends on how wide a range you tested. Report the residual SD in real units alongside it (or instead of it).
  3. Adding terms always raises R². Choose the model from the physics, then check it with residual plots and error on unseen points.
  4. A high R² is not a pass mark. A calibration with R² = 0.999 can still have curved residuals and a wrong shape (Module 2).

What to report instead

The residual SD

In real units: how far a single reading typically sits from the line.

The residual plots

Against the fitted value and in run order. Module 2 shows what to look for.

A range on the slope

The slope with its 95% range, not just the number. Module 6 covers the rest.
