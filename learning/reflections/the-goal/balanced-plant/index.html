The Balanced Plant Theorem

Jonah says a mathematical proof shows that trimming capacity to exactly match demand lowers throughput and sends inventory through the roof. Here is that proof, built up from a first course in probability.

Short answer. The book never gives the proof, and Goldratt cites none. But the claim is a real theorem of queueing theory, and its core fits on half a page (§7–§8). Stated carefully, it says you cannot have both: with random processing times and capacity exactly equal to demand, either inventory grows without bound, or, if you cap inventory, throughput falls strictly below demand.

The interactive parts run in your browser on simulated data. The Python script at the end has the same models, plus an exact solver for longer lines.

§1What exactly is being claimed

Jonah's argument rests on two conditions he names elsewhere in the book: dependent events (a station can only work on what the station before it has finished) and statistical fluctuations (how much a station gets done in a period varies). A "balanced plant" in his sense means every resource has capacity equal to market demand. Nothing is held in reserve.

Two facts make the claim precise. The first is pure bookkeeping. Material is conserved, so for any time \(t\)

$$\text{Output}(t) \;=\; \text{Released}(t) \;-\; \big[\text{WIP}(t) - \text{WIP}(0)\big].$$

If material is released at the demand rate \(d\), then the output shortfall \(d\,t - \mathbb{E}[\text{Output}(t)]\) is the expected growth in work-in-process (WIP). "Throughput goes down" and "inventory goes up" are the same number, read from two ends of the line.

Theorem (what is actually provable). Take a serial line whose stations have random processing times with nonzero variance and mean rates all equal to demand \(d\).

(a) Unlimited buffers: WIP has no steady state. \(\mathbb{E}[\text{WIP}(t)]\) grows without bound, like \(\sqrt{t}\). The long-run output rate still tends to \(d\), but the cumulative shortfall grows like \(\sqrt{t}\) too.

(b) Bounded buffers (or any cap on WIP): the long-run throughput is strictly less than \(d\).

(c) With zero variance, neither happens. The effect comes entirely from randomness.

Goldratt's sentence merges (a) and (b). Over any finite horizon, both effects are present at once, which is what the dice game in §2 shows. §7 proves (a) and §8 proves (b).

§2The matchstick and dice game

On the scout hike, Alex sets up the game with matches and a die. Each player in turn rolls and passes along that many matches, but can't pass more than are sitting in his bowl. The first player draws from an unlimited box. Each die averages 3.5, so a naive plan expects 3.5 matches out per round. Play it below.

Why output falls short, in one inequality. Player \(k\) moves \(\min(X_k, Q_k)\), where \(X_k\) is his roll and \(Q_k\) what's in his bowl. The function \(\min(x, q)\) is concave, so Jensen's inequality gives

$$\mathbb{E}[\min(X_k,Q_k)] \;\le\; \min\!\big(\mathbb{E}X_k,\ \mathbb{E}Q_k\big) \;\le\; 3.5,$$

with strict inequality whenever there's a positive chance that \(Q_k\) is smaller than \(X_k\). A low roll upstream starves the next player, and he can never catch up later: his best roll is capped at 6, and on average he has no spare capacity to make up lost ground. Fluctuations downward propagate, fluctuations upward get clipped.

From a probability course: this is Jensen's inequality with a concave \(\varphi\) instead of the usual convex one, so the inequality points the other way.

§3Toolkit: exponentials and Markov chains

The dice game is hard to solve exactly. Queueing theory picks a model that is solvable and keeps the two essential conditions. You need three ideas.

The exponential distribution and memorylessness

\(S\sim\text{Exp}(\mu)\) has density \(\mu e^{-\mu s}\), mean \(1/\mu\) and variance \(1/\mu^2\). Its key property is memorylessness:

$$P(S > s+t \mid S > s) = \frac{e^{-\mu(s+t)}}{e^{-\mu s}} = e^{-\mu t} = P(S>t).$$

A job that has been on the machine for 10 minutes has the same remaining time as a fresh one. That's unrealistic for machining, but it means the future depends only on how many jobs are where, not on how long each has been there. That is what makes a Markov chain possible.

The Poisson process

If gaps between arrivals are i.i.d. Exp(\(\lambda\)), the number of arrivals in time \(t\) is Poisson(\(\lambda t\)), and in any short interval \(h\) an arrival happens with probability \(\lambda h + o(h)\). We'll treat "arrivals at rate \(\lambda\)" and "service at rate \(\mu\)" as two competing exponential clocks.

Continuous-time Markov chains as a flow balance

A continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) has states and jump rates \(q_{ij}\) between them. Its stationary distribution \(\pi\) is the long-run fraction of time spent in each state. Think of \(\pi_i\) as fluid sitting in tank \(i\), with fluid flowing from \(i\) to \(j\) at rate \(\pi_i q_{ij}\). In steady state, flow in equals flow out at every tank:

$$\pi_j \sum_{k\ne j} q_{jk} \;=\; \sum_{i\ne j} \pi_i\, q_{ij}\quad\text{for all } j, \qquad \textstyle\sum_j \pi_j = 1.$$

That's the mass balance you know from fluids and thermo. For a birth–death chain (states \(0,1,2,\dots\), jumps only to neighbors), there's a shortcut: draw a cut between states \(n\) and \(n+1\). The only flow across it is up at rate \(\pi_n\lambda_n\) and down at rate \(\pi_{n+1}\mu_{n+1}\), so these must match:

$$\pi_n\lambda_n = \pi_{n+1}\mu_{n+1}.$$

Two more facts we'll use. A finite chain in which every state can reach every other (irreducible) has a unique \(\pi\) with every \(\pi_i>0\). An infinite chain may have no \(\pi\) at all. It can be positive recurrent (returns to each state, with finite mean return time; \(\pi\) exists), null recurrent (returns with probability 1, but the mean return time is infinite; no \(\pi\)), or transient (may never return).

Where this is in Ross (Introduction to Probability Models): Ch. 4 for discrete-time chains and recurrence, Ch. 5 for the exponential and Poisson process, Ch. 6 for CTMCs and birth–death chains, Ch. 8 for queueing. Every derivation below is in those chapters.

§4The M/M/1 queue, derived

Kendall notation A/S/c lists the arrival distribution, the service distribution and the number of servers. M means Markovian (exponential), G means general, D means deterministic. So M/M/1 is one machine, Poisson arrivals at rate \(\lambda\), exponential service at rate \(\mu\). The state \(N\) is the number of jobs present (waiting plus in service). Define the utilization \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\).

Stationary distribution

The cut equation with \(\lambda_n=\lambda\), \(\mu_n=\mu\) gives \(\pi_{n+1} = \rho\,\pi_n\), so \(\pi_n = \rho^n\pi_0\). Normalizing needs the geometric series to converge:

$$\sum_{n\ge0}\rho^n\pi_0 = \frac{\pi_0}{1-\rho}=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n,\qquad \text{valid only if }\rho<1.$$

So \(N\) is geometric (counting from 0) with success probability \(1-\rho\). At \(\rho\ge1\) the series diverges and no stationary distribution exists. Keep that in mind for §7.

Mean and variance via the generating function

The probability generating function is the MGF with \(z=e^t\):

$$G(z)=\mathbb{E}[z^N]=\sum_{n\ge0}(1-\rho)(\rho z)^n=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho z}.$$ $$L=\mathbb{E}[N]=G'(1)=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(N)=G''(1)+G'(1)-G'(1)^2=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}.$$

Note the variance grows like \((1-\rho)^{-2}\), faster than the mean. Near saturation the queue isn't just long, it's wildly unpredictable.

Time in system via the tower property

Let \(T\) be a job's time in system. Poisson arrivals see the system in its stationary state (this is called PASTA: Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages). So an arriving job finds \(N_a\sim\pi\). Given \(N_a=n\), it waits for \(n\) jobs and then its own service. The job in service has a fresh Exp(\(\mu\)) remaining time by memorylessness, so \(\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_a=n]=(n+1)/\mu\). Then

$$\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_a]\big]=\frac{L+1}{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\rho}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}.$$ $$\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}[T]&=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_a]\big]=\frac{L+1}{\mu}\\&=\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\rho}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}.\end{aligned}$$
From a probability course: this is the tower property, \(\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_a]]\) (Ross, Ch. 3): condition on what the arrival sees, then average.

§5Little's Law

Check: \(\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[T]=\lambda/(\mu-\lambda)=\rho/(1-\rho)=L\). That isn't a coincidence of the M/M/1 model. It's Little's Law (John D. C. Little, 1961):

$$L=\lambda W\qquad\text{(average number in system = arrival rate × average time in system)}.$$

It holds for essentially any system in which the long-run averages exist: any distributions, any queue discipline, any network. In factory language (Hopp & Spearman write it this way) it reads WIP = TH × CT: work-in-process equals throughput times cycle time.

Equate the two readings over \([0,\tau]\) with \(A(\tau)\) arrivals and divide by \(\tau\):

$$\underbrace{\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau N(t)\,dt}_{\to\,L}\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{A(\tau)}{\tau}}_{\to\,\lambda}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{A(\tau)}\sum_{i\le A(\tau)}T_i}_{\to\,W}\quad(\text{up to edge effects from jobs still inside at }\tau).$$

For Goldratt this means: if throughput is pinned at demand and WIP rises, cycle time (lead time to the customer) rises in proportion. Inventory is never "just" a carrying cost; it is also lateness.

§6Pollaczek–Khinchine and Kingman

Exponential service is a strong assumption. Two named results tell you what happens with other distributions. Both use the squared coefficient of variation (SCV), a unitless measure of variability:

$$c^2 = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\mathbb{E}[X]^2}.\qquad c^2=0\text{ deterministic},\quad c^2=1\text{ exponential},\quad c^2>1\text{ highly variable}.$$

Write \(t_e=\mathbb{E}[S]\) for mean process time, \(u=\lambda t_e\) for utilization, \(c_s^2\) for the SCV of process times and \(c_a^2\) for the SCV of the gaps between arrivals.

Pollaczek–Khinchine (M/G/1, exact)

With Poisson arrivals and any service distribution, the mean wait in queue \(W_q\) (excluding service) is

$$W_q=\frac{\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2(1-u)}=\frac{1+c_s^2}{2}\cdot\frac{u}{1-u}\cdot t_e.$$

Derivation. An arrival waits for the \(N_q\) jobs in queue to be served, plus the residual time \(R\) of the job in service (if any). Arrivals see time averages (PASTA), and service times of queued jobs are independent of how many are queued, so

$$W_q=\mathbb{E}[N_q]\,t_e+u\,\mathbb{E}[R].$$

By Little's Law applied to the queue alone, \(\mathbb{E}[N_q]=\lambda W_q\). The residual time of a randomly inspected service is \(\mathbb{E}[R]=\mathbb{E}[S^2]/(2\,\mathbb{E}[S])\). That's the inspection paradox: a random moment is more likely to land inside a long service than a short one. Substituting gives \(W_q = uW_q + \lambda\mathbb{E}[S^2]/2\), and solving for \(W_q\) gives the formula. Finally \(\mathbb{E}[S^2]=t_e^2(1+c_s^2)\).

Kingman's formula (G/G/1, approximate)

J. F. C. Kingman (1961) showed that as \(u\to1\), the waiting time in a general single-server queue behaves like an exponential random variable with mean approaching

$$\mathbb{E}[W_q]\;\approx\;\underbrace{\left(\frac{c_a^2+c_s^2}{2}\right)}_{V}\;\underbrace{\left(\frac{u}{1-u}\right)}_{U}\;\underbrace{t_e}_{T}.$$

Hopp & Spearman call this the VUT equation: Variability × Utilization × Time. It's exact for M/G/1 (it reduces to Pollaczek–Khinchine when \(c_a^2=1\)) and an approximation otherwise, very good at high utilization. The structure is the whole lesson: waiting is a product. Cutting variability in half has the same effect as a large drop in utilization, and no amount of variability reduction short of zero saves you as \(u\to1\).

Lindley's recursion: the thread that ties it together

For a single first-in-first-out machine, job \(k+1\)'s wait follows from job \(k\)'s:

$$W_{k+1}=\max\big(0,\;W_k+S_k-A_{k+1}\big),$$

where \(A_{k+1}\) is the gap between arrivals. That is a random walk with steps \(S_k-A_{k+1}\), pushed back up whenever it would go below zero (a reflected random walk). The step's mean is \(t_e(1-1/u)\): negative when \(u<1\), which pulls the walk back toward zero and gives a steady state. At \(u=1\) the drift is zero. That's the case §7 handles, and it's exactly the balanced plant.

§7Utilization exactly 1: the random walk

Set \(\lambda=\mu\). The M/M/1 chain now moves up or down with equal probability at each event, bouncing off 0. The cut equations force \(\pi_n=\pi_0\) for all \(n\), which can't sum to 1. There is no stationary distribution. The walk is null recurrent: it returns to 0 with probability 1, but the expected time to do so is infinite, and in between it makes ever longer excursions.

How fast does it grow? Ignoring the floor, \(X(t)=\text{arrivals}-\text{services}\) has mean 0 and variance \((\lambda+\mu)t=2\lambda t\). Reflected at 0, the queue length is \(N(t)=X(t)-\min_{s\le t}X(s)\). By the central limit theorem \(X\) looks like Brownian motion \(\sigma B(t)\) with \(\sigma^2=2\lambda\), and Lévy's theorem says a Brownian motion minus its running minimum has the same distribution as \(|\sigma B(t)|\). So

$$\mathbb{E}[N(t)]\;\approx\;\mathbb{E}|\sigma B(t)|=\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{2t}{\pi}}=2\sqrt{\frac{\lambda t}{\pi}}\;\longrightarrow\;\infty.$$

That proves part (a) of the theorem for one station. A line of stations is a chain of such walks, each feeding the next, and the same \(\sqrt{t}\) growth appears (you saw it in the dice game fit).

§8Finite buffers: throughput falls

Real plants don't have infinite floor space, and good ones cap WIP on purpose. Then the queue can't blow up, and the cost shows up as lost throughput instead. Here's the cleanest case.

Two stations, buffer of size b

Station 1 never starves (unlimited raw material) and runs at rate \(\mu_1\). Station 2 runs at rate \(\mu_2\). Between them is room for \(b\) parts. If station 1 finishes a part and the buffer is full, it holds the part and stops (blocking after service). Track \(n\) = parts finished by station 1 but not yet by station 2, counting the one on station 2 and a blocked part on station 1. Then \(n\in\{0,\dots,b+2\}\), it goes up at rate \(\mu_1\) (unless \(n=b+2\)) and down at rate \(\mu_2\) (unless \(n=0\)). That's a birth–death chain, the M/M/1 queue cut off at \(K=b+2\). The cut equations give \(\pi_n\propto r^n\) with \(r=\mu_1/\mu_2\), and

$$\text{TH}=\mu_2\,(1-\pi_0),\qquad \pi_0=\frac{1-r}{1-r^{K+1}}\;(r\ne1).$$

In the balanced case \(\mu_1=\mu_2=\mu\), every state is equally likely, \(\pi_n=1/(b+3)\), and

$$\boxed{\;\text{TH}=\mu\,\frac{b+2}{b+3}\;}\qquad b=0:\ \tfrac23\mu,\quad b=1:\ \tfrac34\mu,\quad b=10:\ \tfrac{12}{13}\mu.$$ $$\begin{aligned}&\boxed{\;\text{TH}=\mu\,\frac{b+2}{b+3}\;}\\&b=0:\ \tfrac23\mu,\quad b=1:\ \tfrac34\mu,\quad b=10:\ \tfrac{12}{13}\mu.\end{aligned}$$

With no buffer, a balanced two-machine line loses a third of its capacity. Station 2 is starved (\(n=0\)) a fraction \(1/(b+3)\) of the time and station 1 is blocked (\(n=b+2\)) another \(1/(b+3)\).

The general proof of part (b)

For any number of stations with exponential times, the state (each station working, blocked or starved, plus each buffer's count) lives in a finite set. The chain is irreducible, so it has a unique stationary \(\pi\) with every state having positive probability. One of those states has the last station starved. Therefore

$$\text{TH}=\mu_N\big(1-P(\text{last station starved})\big)\;<\;\mu_N=d.$$ $$\begin{aligned}\text{TH}&=\mu_N\big(1-P(\text{last station starved})\big)\\&<\;\mu_N=d.\end{aligned}$$

That's the whole proof, and it's the one Jonah could have meant. The same argument works for general distributions with more machinery (the chain then needs extra coordinates for elapsed times, and "irreducible" becomes a regeneration argument), but the conclusion is identical.

This is the trade-off in one picture. Throughput approaches demand only as buffers, and therefore WIP, go to infinity, which is part (a) again. There is no setting with full throughput and bounded inventory.

§9The bowl phenomenon

If you can't unbalance capacity, you can at least shift work. Frederick Hillier and Ronald Boling (1960s–70s) found that for a fixed total amount of work, a perfectly balanced line isn't the best one. Giving the middle stations slightly less work beats equal shares. Plot throughput against the work allocation and you get an upside-down bowl whose peak is off-center. The reason: a middle station suffers interference from both sides (it can be starved by the station before it and blocked by the one after), while end stations face only one kind.

The size matters as much as the direction. Unbalancing work recovers half a percent. Adding capacity (§10) is where the real gain is, which is Goldratt's actual recommendation.

§10Does the cost claim hold?

Jonah's last step says carrying cost rises, so trimming capacity may not even lower operating expense. That's an empirical claim, but a simple model shows when it's true. Let capacity cost \(c\) per unit of rate per hour and inventory cost \(h\) per unit per hour. For an M/M/1 station,

$$C(\mu)=c\,\mu+h\,\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad C'(\mu)=c-\frac{h\lambda}{(\mu-\lambda)^2}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\mu^*=\lambda+\sqrt{\frac{h\lambda}{c}}.$$

The optimal capacity is demand plus a square-root safety margin (the same square-root rule shows up in call-center staffing and inventory safety stock). Trimming to \(\mu=\lambda\) sends cost to infinity. But if you start above \(\mu^*\), some trimming does save money. So questionable is the right word: the answer depends on where you start relative to \(\mu^*\).

§11What the proof does not say

  • It needs randomness. With deterministic times and synchronized stations, a balanced line runs perfectly. Lean manufacturing's push to cut variability (standard work, setup reduction) is the other way out of the same equation.
  • "Through the roof" is the limiting case. Unbounded growth needs unlimited release and unlimited space. Real plants see high but finite WIP, plus lost throughput, a mix of parts (a) and (b).
  • The magnitude depends on the numbers. How bad it gets scales with c² and buffer size. With low variability and decent buffers, the loss can be a few percent, which a manager might reasonably accept.
  • Exponential models exaggerate. c² = 1 is a lot of variability for a machine. Most machining processes are closer to c² ≈ 0.1–0.5, though breakdowns and setups push effective variability back up (Hopp & Spearman, Ch. 8).
  • The cost step is a model, not a theorem. It depends on the real ratio h/c and on where capacity starts relative to μ*.
  • Goldratt's fix is consistent with the math. Protect the bottleneck with a buffer and give every non-bottleneck protective capacity, the √ margin of §10. Hopp & Spearman's CONWIP (cap total WIP, release one job per completion) is the same idea from the queueing side.

§12Reading list

  1. Start
    Hopp & Spearman, Factory Physics (3rd ed., 2008; Waveland reissue). The book that turned Goldratt's intuitions into equations. Ch. 7 covers Little's Law and best/worst-case lines; Ch. 8 covers variability, the VUT equation and blocking. Written for engineers and managers, not mathematicians. If you read one book, read this.
  2. Rigor
    Ross, Introduction to Probability Models. A standard course text. Ch. 4 (Markov chains), 5 (exponential and Poisson), 6 (CTMCs, birth–death) and 8 (queueing) contain every derivation on this page with full rigor.
  3. Best intro
    Harchol-Balter, Performance Modeling and Design of Computer Systems (Cambridge, 2013). The most readable queueing text there is. Excellent on Little's Law, PASTA and the inspection paradox. The examples are computer systems, but the math is identical.
  4. Transport
    Larson & Odoni, Urban Operations Research (1981). Queueing and spatial models for city services (ambulances, transit, police beats). Free online from MIT. The bridge from factory queues to transportation.
  5. Goldratt
    Goldratt & Fox, The Race (1986). Goldratt's own fuller argument for drum-buffer-rope and protective capacity, with diagrams instead of a novel.
  6. Lines
    Gershwin, Manufacturing Systems Engineering (1994). The rigorous treatment of finite-buffer lines like §8, including decomposition methods for long lines.
  7. Classic
    Kleinrock, Queueing Systems, Vol. 1: Theory (1975). The standard graduate reference. Dense, but complete.
  8. Papers
    Little, "A proof for the queuing formula L = λW," Operations Research (1961), and Little's 50th-anniversary retrospective in the same journal (2011). Kingman, "The single server queue in heavy traffic," Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc. (1961). Spearman, Woodruff & Hopp, "CONWIP: a pull alternative to kanban," IJPR (1990).

§13Where this goes next

  • Reading Your Fits. Queue output is a clean example of the problem in Time-ordered data. Consecutive waiting times are strongly correlated, so a standard error that treats them as independent comes out about 20 times too small at 90% utilization. The last section of the Python script shows the batch-means fix.
  • Operations management. Little's Law and the VUT equation are standard material in a first operations management course, and The Goal is a common assignment there. Factory Physics connects the two.
  • Traffic. Congestion is a queueing problem too. The common BPR travel-time curve, t = t₀[1 + 0.15(V/C)⁴], stays finite at V/C = 1, while Davidson's queueing-based curve, t = t₀[1 + J·x/(1−x)] with x = V/C, blows up exactly like L = ρ/(1−ρ). Which form fits real roads better, and whether the answer differs between rural and urban links, is a question I want to come back to.
