Does a Markov chain forget where it started?

For a two-state chain the whole answer is one number, λ = 1 − a − b. It sets how fast the start is forgotten, and whether it ever is.

Short answer. Yes, at a fixed rate: every step multiplies the starting state's leftover influence by \(|\lambda|\), where \(\lambda = 1-a-b\). There are two exceptions. With \(\lambda = 1\) the chain never moves, so it remembers its start forever. With \(\lambda = -1\) it flips every step and remembers whether the step number is even or odd, again forever. That second chain still spends exactly half its time in each state, which is why "the long-run fraction of time" and "the probability at step n" are different questions.

§1 A machine that is either up or down

Check a machine once per shift. If it is up, it breaks down during the shift with probability \(a\). If it is down, it gets repaired during the shift with probability \(b\). Tomorrow depends only on today, so this is a two-state Markov chain with transition matrix

$$P=\begin{bmatrix}1-a & a\\ b & 1-b\end{bmatrix}\qquad(\text{rows: from up, from down}).$$
Reliability checkUp-times here are geometric with mean \(1/a\) shifts and down-times have mean \(1/b\) shifts. So \(b/(a+b) = \tfrac{1/a}{1/a+1/b}\) is exactly MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR), the steady-state availability. MTTF, the mean up-time, is what many shops call MTBF; strictly, MTBF = MTTF + MTTR for a repaired machine.

Two numbers describe everything that follows:

  • the long-run fraction of time up, \(\pi_{\text{up}} = \dfrac{b}{a+b}\), which is the machine's availability;
  • the memory factor \(\lambda = 1-a-b\). The gap between "probability it's up at shift \(n\)" and \(\pi_{\text{up}}\) shrinks by a factor \(|\lambda|\) every shift.
The settling rule $$\bigl|P(\text{up at shift } n) - \pi_{\text{up}}\bigr| = |\lambda|^n\,\bigl|P(\text{up at shift } 0) - \pi_{\text{up}}\bigr|,$$ so you are within \(\varepsilon\) of the long-run value after \(n \ge \dfrac{\ln(\varepsilon/\text{gap}_0)}{\ln|\lambda|}\) shifts (for \(0 < |\lambda| < 1\) and \(\varepsilon < \text{gap}_0\); if \(\lambda = 0\) it takes one step).
Time constant\(|\lambda|^n = e^{-n/\tau}\) with \(\tau = -1/\ln|\lambda|\). It is the same idea as a first-order system's time constant: about \(4.6\,\tau\) steps to get within 1% of the starting gap.

Example. With \(a = 0.05\) (a breakdown about every 20 shifts) and \(b = 0.30\) (about 3.3 shifts to repair), \(\pi_{\text{up}} = 0.30/0.35 = 0.857\) and \(\lambda = 0.65\). From the worst start (down, a gap of 0.857), the probability is within 0.01 of 0.857 after \(\ln(0.01/0.857)/\ln 0.65 = 10.3\), so 11 shifts. After that, which state you started in no longer matters for planning.

§2 Try it

Move \(a\) and \(b\), or pick a preset. "P(up at step n)" follows the probability of being up from each of the two possible starts. "Distance from the long-run value" shows the gap on a log scale: a straight line there means geometric decay, and its slope is \(\ln|\lambda|\), the way you'd read a decay rate off a semilog plot in the lab.

§3 Why it works: a first-order system

Write \(p_n = P(\text{up at step } n)\). To be up at step \(n+1\), the machine was either up and didn't fail, or down and got repaired:

$$p_{n+1} = (1-a)\,p_n + b\,(1-p_n) = \lambda\,p_n + b.$$

That is a first-order linear difference equation. Its fixed point solves \(\pi = \lambda\pi + b\), so \(\pi = b/(1-\lambda) = b/(a+b)\). Subtract the two equations and the constant drops out:

$$p_{n+1}-\pi = \lambda\,(p_n-\pi)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_n-\pi = \lambda^n\,(p_0-\pi).$$
In signals terms\(\lambda\) is the discrete-time pole. A pole on the positive real axis decays smoothly, one on the negative axis alternates (the "Rings" preset), and a pole on the unit circle at \(\pm 1\) never decays.

That's the settling rule from §1. The same fact in matrix form, which is how it generalizes: let \(\Pi\) be the matrix whose two rows are both \(\pi = (\pi_{\text{up}}, \pi_{\text{down}})\). One line of algebra checks that \(P = \Pi + \lambda\,(I-\Pi)\), that \(\Pi^2 = \Pi\), and that \(\Pi(I-\Pi) = 0\). Since \(\Pi\) and \(I - \Pi\) are complementary projections, powers of \(P\) only power the scalar in front:

$$P^n = \Pi + \lambda^n\,(I-\Pi).$$

Row "up" of \(P^n\) is the distribution at step \(n\) starting up, row "down" is the one starting down, and the term \(\lambda^n(I-\Pi)\) is the whole of the memory. Levin and Peres work exactly this two-state example in their first section; the general statement is in Norris (references below).

§4 When it never forgets

\(|\lambda| = 1\) happens in only two ways, and they fail differently.

  • \(\lambda = 1\) (\(a = b = 0\)): the chain never moves. Every distribution is stationary, and the chain stays in whatever state it started in. The long-run fraction of time up is 1 or 0 depending on the start.
  • \(\lambda = -1\) (\(a = b = 1\)): the chain flips every step. \(\pi = (\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) is stationary and the chain spends exactly half its time in each state, yet \(P(\text{up at step } n)\) alternates 1, 0, 1, 0 forever. It has a perfect memory of parity.
Engineering analogyA square wave has a perfectly good DC average, ½ of its peak, but no limit. The time average and the limit are different operations.

So "the fraction of time spent up converges" and "the probability of being up converges" are separate claims. The first holds for any chain that can get from every state to every other (the ergodic theorem). The second also needs the chain to be aperiodic, which the flip chain is not. Below, the running fraction of time up settles to ½ for the flip chain while the step-by-step probability never does.

§5 Bigger chains

Practical useFor a bigger chain, the settling rule still works with \(|\lambda_2|\), the second-largest eigenvalue modulus .

The two-state result is the simplest case of a general fact. Every transition matrix has 1 as an eigenvalue, and every other eigenvalue has modulus at most 1. For a chain that is irreducible and aperiodic, the others are strictly inside the unit circle, and the one with the largest modulus sets the eventual rate at which \(P^n\) approaches \(\Pi\) (exactly, if \(P\) is diagonalizable; otherwise up to a polynomial factor), the way \(\lambda\) does here. For two states the second eigenvalue is just \(1-a-b\). Levin and Peres build mixing times on this idea: their Chapter 12 is on eigenvalues, and for reversible chains they define the relaxation time as \(1/(1-|\lambda_2|)\). Every two-state chain is reversible, so here it is \(1/(1-|\lambda|)\).

In practice this tells you how long a simulation's warm-up has to be before its starting state stops mattering, and how far apart two observations must be before they are nearly independent.
