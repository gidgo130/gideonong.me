How big does n have to be?

Reading skewness from the central limit theorem: a sample-size rule you can use today, what to do when you can't meet it, and why the shape of your data sets it.

Short answer. For the usual 95% interval on a mean, \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt n\), Sugden, Smith & Jones (2000) recommend

$$n \;>\; 28 + 25\,\gamma_1^2,$$

where \(\gamma_1\) is the skewness of the thing you're measuring. Symmetric data need about 30. Exponential-looking data (failure times, waiting times) need about 130. If you can't get that many, or you need a one-sided bound, above all an upper bound on right-skewed data ("mean repair time is at most…"), use a bootstrap-t interval. The rule comes from the CLT's correction terms. Skewness enters at order \(1/\sqrt n\) and shifts each tail. In a two-sided interval those shifts cancel, and what's left at order \(1/n\) involves the skewness squared, which is why the rule grows like \(\gamma_1^2\).

§1 The rule, and how to use it

"\(n \ge 30\)" is the rule most of us were handed. It ignores the one property of the data that matters most here, the skewness \(\gamma_1 = \mathbb E[(X-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\): zero for a symmetric spread, positive for a long right tail.

Where the 28 comes fromFor perfectly normal data, \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt n\) covers only 91.8% at \(n = 10\) and is still just under 94% at \(n = 29\). The 28 is the price of using 1.96 with an estimated \(s\). Use the t critical value (Excel's CONFIDENCE.T ) and that part goes away (exactly, for normal data); the \(25\gamma_1^2\) does not.
The rule (Sugden, Smith & Jones 2000, eq. 4.3)

Trust a two-sided 95% interval for a mean when \(n > 28 + 25\gamma_1^2\). "Trust" means the interval really covers the mean at least 94% of the time.

How to use it

  1. Estimate the skewness. Excel: =SKEW(A2:A41) . Treat the result as rough: at \(n = 30\) it scatters by about ±0.4 even for symmetric data, and by about ±0.7 for exponential data, where it also averages about 1.6 instead of 2. If the physics says the quantity is bounded at zero with a long right tail (times, sizes, concentrations), believe the physics.
  2. Compare with the rule. \(n\) comfortably above \(28 + 25\gamma_1^2\): the usual interval is fine.
  3. If you're short, use a bootstrap-t interval . Excel has no built-in bootstrap. Taking logs first also helps, but then the interval is for the geometric mean (the median, for lognormal data), not the mean.
  4. One-sided statements need more care, and which side depends on the skew. For right-skewed data (times, sizes, concentrations) the interval sits too low, so an upper bound ("mean repair time is at most…") is the one that fails, while a lower bound is conservative. For left-skewed data, such as the strength of a mature process (Weibull with shape above about 3.6), it is the reverse. §2 and §4 show that one tail goes wrong long before the two-sided coverage does.

§2 Check it: what really happens to coverage

Pick a kind of data and simulate many samples of each size. For every sample, build the 95% interval and record whether the true mean fell above it (the interval came out too low) or below it. Each should happen 2.5% of the time.

Engineering readingFor right-skewed data the interval is too low more often than too high. If you are claiming a lower bound on mean life, you're probably fine; if you're claiming an upper bound on mean time to repair, you are the one being fooled.

Three things to try. With exponential data at the rule's \(n = 128\), total coverage is about 94%, as promised, but the two tails are lopsided: about 4.3% of intervals land too low and about 1.35% too high. With uniform data the t interval is on target almost immediately. And switching to 1.96 makes small samples worse for every kind of data: that's the 28.

§3 Why: the CLT has a next term

Standardize the sample mean, \(Y_n = (\bar X_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\). The CLT says its distribution approaches the standard normal. It says nothing about how fast, and the speed is where the skewness shows up.

Decibels for distributionsMoment generating functions of independent pieces multiply, the way transfer functions of cascaded stages do. Cumulants are their logarithms, so they add, the way gains in dB add.

Cumulants are the bookkeeping tool. The third one is the skewness, the fourth is the excess kurtosis \(\gamma_2\). They add when you add independent variables and scale like \(\kappa_r(aX) = a^r\kappa_r(X)\), which gives the one fact this page rests on:

$$\text{skewness of } \bar X_n = \frac{\gamma_1}{\sqrt n},\qquad \text{excess kurtosis of } \bar X_n = \frac{\gamma_2}{n}.$$ $$\begin{aligned}\text{skewness of } \bar X_n &= \frac{\gamma_1}{\sqrt n},\\ \text{excess kurtosis of } \bar X_n &= \frac{\gamma_2}{n}.\end{aligned}$$

Each extra order fades half a power of \(n\) faster. Translating the cumulants back into a CDF gives the Edgeworth expansion; for a parent with a finite third moment that isn't confined to evenly spaced values (Feller 1971, §XVI.4; Hall 1992, Ch. 2),

$$F_n(x) = \Phi(x) \;-\; \frac{\gamma_1}{6\sqrt n}\,(x^2-1)\,\varphi(x) \;+\; o\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\right),$$ $$\begin{aligned}F_n(x) &= \Phi(x) - \frac{\gamma_1}{6\sqrt n}(x^2-1)\varphi(x)\\&\quad+ o(1/\sqrt n),\end{aligned}$$

where \(F_n\) is the CDF of \(Y_n\) and \(\varphi\), \(\Phi\) are the normal density and CDF. With a finite fourth moment and a parent smooth enough to have a density (Cramér's condition), the next term is of order \(1/n\); it brings in \(\gamma_2\) and \(\gamma_1^2\) and is in the same references. Read the first correction three ways:

  • Size. Its largest value is \(0.0665\,|\gamma_1|/\sqrt n\) (at \(x = 0\)). Quadrupling \(n\) only halves it.
  • Shape. \((x^2-1)\varphi(x)\) is an even bump. For a right-skewed parent the median of \(\bar X\) sits below its mean, so \(F_n(0) > \tfrac12\).
  • Reading it backwards. Measure the error and you have the skewness: \(\gamma_1 = 6\sqrt{2\pi}\,\lim_n \sqrt n\,\bigl(F_n(0)-\tfrac12\bigr)\). The speed of the CLT is a fingerprint of the parent.

What to look for. The exponential error is the even bump, matched by the one-term line, with the two-term line closing the rest; its largest error times \(\sqrt n\) settles at 0.133, and \(0.133 \times 6/\varphi(0) = 2.00\), its skewness. The uniform has \(\gamma_1 = 0\), so the one-term line is flat and what's left is an odd wiggle, 15 times smaller at \(n = 10\), that shrinks like \(1/n\). The coin is symmetric too, yet its error never shrinks faster than \(1/\sqrt n\) (§5).

§4 Why two-sided intervals forgive skewness

The arithmetic in the paperWith \(\gamma_2 = 0\), an infinite population and 1.96 with \(s\), Sugden et al. get the \(1/n\) coefficient \(-2.3724 - 2.1170\gamma_1^2\) at 1.96. Each tail of the interval is off by about \(\varphi(1.96)(2.3724 + 2.1170\gamma_1^2)/n\), so the total coverage error is \((0.2773 + 0.2474\gamma_1^2)/n\). Asking for less than 1% gives \(n > 27.73 + 24.74\gamma_1^2\), rounded to the rule.

A two-sided interval asks for \(P(|Y_n| \le z) = F_n(z) - F_n(-z)\). The one-term correction is even in \(x\), so it is the same at \(z\) and \(-z\) and cancels in the difference. What's left is order \(1/n\), with a coefficient that contains \(\gamma_1^2\). Ask for that error to be under 1% and you get \(n\) proportional to \(\gamma_1^2\). That is the shape of the rule.

Each tail on its own gets no such cancellation. For the t-statistic the one-term polynomial changes from \(-(x^2-1)\) to \((2x^2+1)\) (Hall 1992, Ch. 2; Sugden et al., eq. 2.4), and at 1.96 it moves each tail by about

$$\pm\,0.085\,\frac{\gamma_1}{\sqrt n}.$$

For exponential data at \(n = 128\) that is about 1.5 percentage points, the lopsidedness you saw in §2. A one-sided bound feels all of it, which is why the rule is about two-sided intervals only.

Why bootstrap-t fixes it. Resampling your own data reproduces that \(1/\sqrt n\) term, estimated from your sample's skewness, so the bootstrap-t interval's one-sided error drops to order \(1/n\) (Hall 1992, Ch. 3). It uses the very correction the CLT throws away.

§5 What else the speed gives away

Allowable versus typicalBerry–Esseen is like a design allowable: guaranteed, and conservative by a factor of about 9 here (7 with Shevtsova's sharper form). The Edgeworth term is the typical value. You need both, for different jobs.
  • Evenly spaced values never beat \(1/\sqrt n\). A coin, counts, readings rounded to an instrument's resolution: the CDF of the mean is a staircase, and a staircase can't follow a smooth ramp better than half a step. For the fair coin the error stays near \(0.4/\sqrt n\) however symmetric it is (Esseen 1945; Feller 1971, §XVI.4). The continuity correction, adding half a step, is the fix.
  • Heavy tails converge more slowly. If the share of the variance coming from values beyond \(z\) shrinks only like \(z^{-\delta}\), with \(0 < \delta < 1\), the error shrinks like \(n^{-\delta/2}\), and the converse holds too (Ibragimov 1966). Fatigue lives, flood levels and network traffic can behave like this. A quick check: if two or three points contribute most of \(\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2\), distrust any CLT-based interval.
  • Infinite variance: no normal limit at all. The average of \(n\) Cauchy readings is Cauchy again, exactly as spread out as one reading (Durrett 2019, §3.8, on stable laws). A Lorentzian resonance line is a Cauchy density. If averaging more data doesn't narrow anything, suspect this.
  • A guaranteed bound for every n. The Berry–Esseen theorem: \(\sup_x |F_n(x)-\Phi(x)| \le 0.4748\,\rho/\sqrt n\), with \(\rho = \mathbb E|X-\mu|^3/\sigma^3\) (Berry 1941; Esseen 1942; Shevtsova 2011 for the constant). For exponential data that's \(1.15/\sqrt n\) against a true \(0.133/\sqrt n\). Shevtsova also gives a sharper form, \(0.33554(\rho + 0.415)/\sqrt n\): \(0.95/\sqrt n\) for exponential data, about 7 times the true error.
